# Chinese

The Chinese corpus - official translation - is here: 25 masters translated from
the Russian masters in `ru/`, plus the Manifesto in `_manifest/zh-manifest.md`.

Translated from Russian, not from English, and not from the earlier round of
Chinese pages that still sits on the site. Those pages predate the rewriting of
the Russian masters - the Declaration was rebuilt into five parts and eleven
articles - and they are not a reference text.

Chinese is one of the authentic languages of the UN Charter and of both
Covenants, so legal terms here are quoted from the originals rather than
reconstructed. That brings a trap no other language in this corpus has: **there
are two Chinese texts of the Covenants.** The authentic text of 1966, deposited
with the Secretary-General, is written in literary Chinese - the Covenant is
called 盟约 there and sub-paragraphs are marked 子 / 丑 / 寅. The modern PRC
translation, which Chinese courts and scholarship actually cite, calls it 公约
and marks sub-paragraphs 甲 / 乙 / 丙. It is not the authentic text of the treaty.
The rule this corpus follows: quotations come from the authentic text word for
word, our own prose is written in modern Chinese, and where the two diverge the
divergence is recorded in the decision file, not smoothed over and not explained
inside the document.

Every terminology decision, with its source, is recorded in
`_СЛОВАРЬ_РЕШЕНИЙ.md` in this directory. The decisions that had no canonical
source and had to be built are collected there separately and marked `[СЛАБОЕ]`:
those are the ones a native reader should look at first.

Three Chinese-specific points are set out in that file and matter more than they
look. A people is 人民 and a nation is 民族, and the two are never swapped -
in the language of Chinese public law the difference decides who is speaking.
Our Charter is 宪章 and never 章程, because 章程 is the constitutive instrument of
a registered organisation, exactly what Article 4 of the Declaration denies that
we are. And a collective will is 共同意志, never 公意: the latter is Rousseau's
general will as Chinese philosophy renders it, a term with an argument already
built into it.

Two further distinctions are load-bearing, and both were nearly lost. Burning a
passport is 销毁; annulling an invalid issue is 注销. The Russian master keeps
them apart - in one document it uses both inside a single sentence - and eight
of thirteen translating agents collapsed them into 注销 anyway. They are
separated again, in eight documents. And 权力 and 权利, which sound identical and
differ by one character, are kept apart everywhere: 权利 is a subjective right
and is our register, 权力 is public power, which Article 9 of the Declaration
says this people does not exercise. 权力 stays only in quotations about states
and inside prohibitions - "power does not accumulate" - where it is the very
thing being forbidden.

One defect in the UN source is recorded rather than reproduced: in the Chinese
text of the Covenant on Civil and Political Rights published on `un.org` and
`ohchr.org`, a Latin `n` stands in place of a character in Articles 12(3), 18(3)
and 22(2) - 公共n生 for 公共卫生. No quotation in this corpus touches those
paragraphs; if one ever does, the reconstruction is marked as such.

What still needs a native Chinese reader: the voice texts - the Manifesto, 03,
17, 23, 31. The legal documents are closed by the accuracy of citation and are
verifiable without a native speaker; intonation is not.

The Manifesto PDF is not built for Chinese: the generator has no CJK font
subset. The web page and the corpus are complete without it.
